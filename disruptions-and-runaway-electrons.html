Disruptions, Runaway Electrons, and Electromagnetic Loads in a Compact Spherical-Tokamak Breeder: A Reduced-Order Assessment on a NIMROD-Verified Equilibrium

The disruption response is the credibility gap a compact spherical-tokamak program is expected to answer.

Disruptionsrunaway electronsSPI required

Abstract

The disruption response is the credibility gap a compact spherical-tokamak program is expected to answer. We assess it for the frozen breeder design point (\(I_p=9.66\) MA) with a reduced-order model built on a NIMROD-verified equilibrium, and we report the honest, and uncomfortable, result. The current-quench time is 7-19 ms, which sets the required mitigation-trigger speed. The runaway-electron avalanche is SEVERE: with the electric field far above the critical field (\(E/E_c1128\)), a milliampere seed amplifies to a multi-megaampere runaway beam carrying of order \(70 %\) of the plasma current. The halo current is 35 % of the plasma current, which sets the mechanical load basis for the vacuum vessel and first wall. The load-bearing conclusion is unambiguous and is carried as a hard design requirement: shattered-pellet (or equivalent) disruption mitigation is mandatory, and its trigger must act within the current-quench window. We state the method's limits — this is a reduced-order assessment on a verified equilibrium, not a full dynamic disruption simulation — but the direction is robust, and we explicitly do not claim the disruption risk is negligible.

Introduction

A compact, high-current spherical tokamak stores a large magnetic and thermal energy in a small volume, and a disruption releases it fast. Reviewers rightly expect a compact-ST program to answer the disruption question directly: how fast is the current quench, how severe is the runaway-electron avalanche, and what electromagnetic loads must the vessel survive. We answer these for the frozen design point with a reduced-order model on a NIMROD-verified equilibrium, and we do not soften the result.

Method

A disruption terminates the plasma current on a current-quench time \(\tau_{CQ}=7-19\) ms, inducing a toroidal electric field that can seed and multiply runaway electrons. Runaways are born above the Connor–Hastie critical field \[E_c=\frac{n_e e^3\ln\Lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0^2\,m_e c^2},\] and the seed then grows by close-collision avalanche, \[\frac{dn_{RE}}{dt}=\frac{n_{RE}}{\tau_a},\qquad \tau_a\propto\frac{\ln\Lambda}{(E/E_c-1)},\] so the runaway population amplifies as \(\exp\!\big(\!\int dt/\tau_a\big)\); in a compact, high-current spherical tokamak the number of e-foldings is large, which is why the avalanche risk is graded SEVERE and the mitigation requirement — not the equilibrium — sets the design margin. The halo-current fraction reaches 35 %, fixing the vessel-force specification.

The current-quench time is estimated from an inductance-over-resistance model on a NIMROD-verified free-boundary equilibrium at the design current \(I_p=9.66\) MA. The runaway-electron avalanche is characterized by the ratio of the toroidal electric field to the critical field \(E/E_c\) and the resulting avalanche gain from a seed current. The halo current is taken as the fraction of plasma current flowing in the scrape-off/halo region during a vertical displacement, and provides the mechanical load basis for the vessel and first wall. The model is reduced-order: it establishes magnitudes and the mitigation requirement, not a time-resolved dynamic disruption.

Current quench

The current-quench time is 7-19 ms. This window sets the mitigation-trigger speed: a disruption-mitigation system must sense and act well inside it.

Runaway-electron avalanche

The avalanche is SEVERE. The toroidal electric field sits far above the critical field (\(E/E_c\approx1128\)), so a milliampere-scale seed amplifies through the avalanche to a multi-megaampere runaway beam carrying of order \(70\,\%\) of the plasma current. A runaway beam of this magnitude, if it strikes the first wall, is a component-damaging event — which is precisely why mitigation is a requirement rather than an option.

Electromagnetic loads

The halo current is 35 % of the plasma current. This is the load basis the vacuum vessel and first-wall mechanical design must react during a vertical displacement event, and it couples to the structural assessment of the machine backbone.

Discussion

The three results combine into a single design imperative. The current quench is fast enough that mitigation must be triggered quickly; the runaway avalanche is severe enough that an unmitigated disruption threatens the first wall; and the halo-current load is large enough to size vessel structure. Shattered-pellet injection (or an equivalent massive-material-injection scheme) is therefore a hard design requirement for this machine, with a trigger inside the current-quench window. This is a demanding but standard requirement for a high-current tokamak, and naming it is part of answering the disruption question honestly.

Limitations

This is a reduced-order assessment on a NIMROD-verified equilibrium, not a full time-resolved dynamic disruption or runaway-electron transport simulation; a dynamic NIMROD/DREAM-class study is the natural next step and would refine the current-quench band, the avalanche gain, and the halo-current distribution. The \(E/E_c\) and avalanche figures are model estimates that establish severity, not a converged runaway-current prediction. The mitigation requirement is robust to these refinements; the precise numbers are not final. We explicitly do not claim the disruption risk is negligible — the opposite is the finding.

Conclusion

For the frozen breeder design point (\(I_p=9.66\) MA) the current quench is 7-19 ms, the runaway-electron avalanche is SEVERE (\(E/E_c\approx1128\), a runaway beam of order \(70\,\%\) of \(I_p\)), and the halo current is 35 % of the plasma current. The result is a hard design requirement: shattered-pellet-class disruption mitigation, triggered within the current-quench window. The disruption question is answered directly, and its answer is a requirement, not a reassurance.
