MetroVolt HVDC-native power interface

Abstract

A fusion generator built around direct energy conversion is not an alternating-current machine dressed as one: it is intrinsically a high-voltage direct-current source, and that changes the electrical interface to the load. We describe the power-electronics consequences for the MetroVolt data-center housing. About \(54 %\) of the gross output leaves the direct-energy converter as direct current at \(50\)–\(250\) kV, with no synchronous generator and no grid-frequency stage; reaching an \(800\) V direct-current server-rack bus takes two to three power-electronic conversion stages, against four to five for a conventional alternating-current plant feeding the same bus. The same architecture enables fast electronic load-following: a direct converter can modulate its output at millisecond scale, whereas following the load by throttling the fusion plasma is uniquely expensive because a net sensitivity amplifier of \(4.14\) means a \(10 %\) plasma dip cuts net export by \(41 %\). Load-following should therefore be done on the electronic path with dump and storage, not on the plasma. This is a power-electronics and electrical-interface result; economics, siting, and service-level content are held internal and excluded. Introduction

Most fusion power-plant studies assume an alternating-current turbine hall. A direct-energy-conversion machine does not have one: its converters produce direct current at high voltage. When the load is a data center — itself increasingly a direct-current bus — the electrical interface is shorter and different, and it is worth stating precisely.

An HVDC-native source

A direct energy converter is an HVDC-native source: it collects the axial charged-particle flux on biased grids that decelerate the ions, turning directed kinetic energy into terminal voltage without a thermal cycle. The recovered electrical power is the collected current times the collector potential, tiered to the ion spectrum, \[P_{\mathrm{DC}}=\sum_j I_j\,V_j,\qquad \eta_{\mathrm{DEC}}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{DC}}}{P_{\mathrm{beam}}}.\] At the design spectrum the converter efficiency is \(\eta_{\mathrm{DEC}}=0.7\). Because the output is already DC at grid-relevant voltage, load-following is handled in the power electronics — not by modulating the plasma — as the section below shows.

Figure shows the split (gate MV-L1-A3): about \(54\,\%\) of the gross output is delivered by the direct-energy converter as direct current at \(50\)–\(250\) kV, with the remainder from a thermal bottoming cycle. There is no synchronous generator and no grid-frequency conversion stage on the direct path. Reaching an \(800\) V direct-current rack bus therefore takes two to three power-electronic stages, against four to five for a conventional alternating-current plant that must first synchronize to grid frequency and then rectify back to direct current at the rack.

Load-following: electronics, not plasma

The direct path also sets how the plant follows a varying load. A direct converter modulates at millisecond scale, limited by its power electronics. Following the load by throttling the plasma is far more costly: the gross-to-net sensitivity amplifier is \(4.14\), so a \(10\,\%\) dip in plasma output cuts net export by \(\approx41\,\%\) (gate MV-L1-A1). The design implication is explicit — load-following is done on the electronic path with dump resistors and storage, holding the plasma at its efficient operating point, not by modulating the fusion source.

Limitations

The conversion-stage counts and the direct-current fraction are architecture-level results at the design point; detailed converter efficiencies, protection coordination, and storage sizing are engineering follow-ons. The gross/net amplifier assumes the frozen power balance. Economics, siting, waste-heat revenue, and service-level commitments are internal-only and are excluded here by design.

Conclusion

The MetroVolt generator is HVDC-native: \(\approx54\,\%\) of output leaves the direct-energy converter as \(50\)–\(250\) kV direct current, reaching an \(800\) V rack bus in two to three stages rather than four to five, and load-following belongs on the millisecond electronic path, not on the plasma (whose \(4.14\) net amplifier makes throttling expensive).
